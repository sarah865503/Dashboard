✨Executive summary

1. Remote developers earn more than hybrid developers. Typical remote salaries average $98.7k vs. $79.9k for hybrid. Five independent methods (t-tests, bootstrap, confidence intervals, median tests) all agree the gap is real, not chance.

2. Education matters, but less than you might expect. In the US and Canada, Bachelor's holders earn about $17-18k less than Master's or Professional-degree holders, who earn the same as each other. Education explains only 1.5% of pay differences.

3. Use with care. This is a self-selected survey: the remote premium may reflect seniority, role or location rather than remote work itself. Use it to benchmark pay, not as proof that remote work causes higher salaries.

🏠Remote vs. hybrid salaries

Descriptive statistics (USD)

A few extreme salaries (up to $33.6M, many likely entry errors) would distort the averages, so we removed outliers with a standard rule. Afterwards the typical remote respondent earns about $13k more (median $87.7k vs. $74.9k).

Two-sample t-test: manual vs. built-in

✓ Independence (separate respondents; self-selected survey)✓ Normality (thousands per group)✗ Equal variances (Levene)✓ Welch test agrees
Our hand calculation and the software give identical results (|t| = 19.10). The p-value says that if pay were truly equal, a gap this large would almost never appear by chance. It does not tell us how big the gap matters or what causes it. The groups have unequal spread, but the alternative test that allows for this reaches the same answer.

Bootstrap: 10,000 re-samples

We re-sampled the survey 10,000 times to see how much the averages wobble. The two groups' averages never overlap and the gap never crosses zero. The standardized view puts the result on the t-test scale (about 19 standard errors from zero).

Bootstrap t-test and confidence intervals

We are 95% confident that remote workers earn $16.9k-$20.7k more on average; the formula and bootstrap intervals differ by only about $10 and both exclude zero. A t-test on the bootstrap data gives a huge statistic by construction (it grows with the number of re-samples), so treat it as a yes/no that confirms the same conclusion.

Median comparison

Medians tell the same story and are not swayed by extremes: the typical remote developer earns $12.9k more. Both tests reject "no difference"; the bootstrap p-value cannot go below 1 in 10,000, which is why the two p-values look different in size.

One-way ANOVA

ANOVA asks whether at least one education group differs. It does (p = 1.4×10-14), but education explains only 1.5% of pay variation. Spread is unequal, yet a test that does not assume equal spread (Kruskal-Wallis) agrees.

Bootstrap by education level

Pick a pair above. Bachelor's holders earn about $17.7k less than Master's and $17.1k less than Professional-degree holders (their gaps sit far from zero). Master's and Professional holders are statistically indistinguishable (centre near zero). ANOVA on the bootstrap data gives an inflated F by construction, so rely on the original-data result.

🧪What-if lab

Change the inputs and watch the answers move. Built only from the survey's aggregated results.

1. How confident do you need to be?

Demanding more certainty widens the range of plausible gaps. Even at 99% the range stays well away from zero, so the remote premium is not a fluke. At 95% these match the intervals above.

2. Where does a salary sit?

Slide to test an offer or a pay band: it shows what share of each group earns less. The same salary ranks higher among hybrid than remote respondents, which is the pay gap seen from another angle.

3. Sample size and gap simulator

Small gaps can look like noise in small samples. Drag respondents down to about 100 per group: the survey's $18.8k gap becomes much harder to confirm. Our survey had about 7,000 per group, which is why the result is so clear. (Normal approximation; the exact survey t-test is in section 2.)

4. Run your own bootstrap

Each re-sample draws about 7,400 hybrid and 6,730 remote salaries with replacement, averages each group and records the gap; watch the histogram fill in. Few re-samples give a jumpy answer, thousands give a stable one (compare the "last runs" table). The notebook used 10,000 re-samples of the raw responses; this live demo re-samples from a 101-point summary of each group, so it lands close to, but not exactly on, the notebook figures.
